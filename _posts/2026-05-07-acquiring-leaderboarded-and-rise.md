Last year I bought a competitor's domain. This year I bought another one. Two former leaderboard tools, both shut down, both now back online — but serving very different audiences than they used to.

## The first one: Leaderboarded.com

A few months before I [split KeepTheScore.com in two](/blog/splitting-keepthescore/), [Leaderboarded.com](https://leaderboarded.com) — one of my competitors — shut down. They actually started recommending KeepTheScore to their users on the way out, which was a generous gesture and one I still appreciate.

I bought the domain. Not the user list, not the codebase. Just the name.

A short while later I needed to split my own product. Sports scoreboards stay at [KeepTheScore.com](https://keepthescore.com), where they belong — that's the platform built for parents, youth leagues, and community broadcasters streaming local games. Everything else — sales leaderboards, classroom gamification, fundraising thermometers, goal trackers, scoresheets — moves to [Leaderboarded.com](https://leaderboarded.com), which is now a focused B2B leaderboard tool for sales managers, team leads, and event organizers.

Without the acquired domain, that split would have been much harder. I'd have had to invent a brand, register something nobody had ever heard of, and start from zero on SEO and brand recall. With Leaderboarded.com, search engines already understood the topic, the name was on-the-nose, and former Leaderboarded users searching for the brand landed on a working product instead of a 404.

That worked out well. The split is now several months old and Leaderboarded has its own ICP, its own pricing, and its own roadmap — not just KTS with a different logo.

## The second one: Rise.global

Rise.global was another former leaderboard tool that shut down around the same time. Similar story: real customers, real history, and a domain that was sitting unused.

I'm relaunching Rise as something different from either KeepTheScore or Leaderboarded: a [tournament bracket maker](https://rise.global), with live scoreboards, streaming overlays, and custom themes. The bracket use case never quite fit either of my existing products — sports brackets have a streaming dimension that doesn't fit Leaderboarded's B2B audience, and they're too distinct from a running-score scoreboard to live comfortably under KeepTheScore. Rise.global is the natural place for it. Once again, the domain comes with built-in topical authority.

It's still in development. [Join the waitlist](https://rise.global) if it's interesting.

## So now I have four products

[KeepTheScore.com](https://keepthescore.com) for sports scoreboards. [Leaderboarded.com](https://leaderboarded.com) for business and education leaderboards. [Scorejudge.com](https://scorejudge.com) for [competition judging](/blog/score-judge/). And Rise.global for tournament brackets, coming soon.

Four products from one founder feels like a lot. It's possible because of two things working in tandem.

First: AI does most of the typing now. Splitting KeepTheScore took about 10 days. That used to be a months-long project. Each of these products would have been a year of work three years ago.

Second: each product has a distinct ICP. KTS is sports parents and youth leagues. Leaderboarded is sales managers and team leads. Scorejudge is hackathon and event organizers. Rise will be tournament producers. Same underlying tech, four positioning angles. Multi-tenant from a backend perspective; four brands from a marketing perspective.

## On buying competitor domains

The unusual move is treating dead competitor domains as a viable acquisition strategy. The conventional wisdom is to build a new brand. The contrarian case is that for any niche older than a few years, there are dead products with domains sitting unused — often available for low five-figure sums. Each one comes with topical authority, brand recall among former users, and sometimes a pre-built audience.

It's not a strategy I'd have predicted when I started KeepTheScore in 2017. But it's worked out twice now, and I'd do it again.
